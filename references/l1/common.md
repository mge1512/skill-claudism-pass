# Interference shared across European first languages

Read this file first, then the file for the writer's first language. These items
turn up in nearly every European L1 and cause most of the damage.

## The two words that mislead almost everyone

- **eventually**. In German (eventuell), Czech (eventuálně), Dutch (eventueel),
  French (éventuellement), Spanish (eventualmente), Italian (eventualmente) and
  Greek the cognate means *possibly*. English "eventually" means *in the end*.
  A sentence like "we will eventually support ARM" promises delivery where the
  writer meant "possibly". In a roadmap or a contract this is expensive.
  Replace with "possibly", "if needed", or "in the end", whichever was meant.
- **actual / actually**. The cognate means *current* or *currently* almost
  everywhere on the continent. English "actual" means *real*, "actually" means
  *in fact*. Replace with "current", "currently", "at the moment".

## Uncountable nouns

English has no plural for: information, software, hardware, feedback, advice,
research, training, staff, equipment, evidence, progress, knowledge, work.
Write "information", "two pieces of feedback", "training sessions", "staff
members". "Informations", "softwares", "trainings", "feedbacks", "advices" and
"researches" are wrong in every register.

## Time expressions

- "since three years" -> "for three years". "Since" takes a point in time
  (since 2019), "for" takes a duration.
- "I work here since 2019" -> "I have worked here since 2019". Most European
  languages use the present tense where English needs the present perfect.
- "until Friday" for a deadline -> "by Friday". "Until" means the activity
  continues up to that point; "by" means it is finished at that point.

## Questions and word order

- "How does it look like?" -> "What does it look like?" or "How does it look?"
  Mixing the two is one of the most recognisable non-native constructions.
- English word order is fixed: subject, verb, object. Fronting an adverbial does
  not invert the verb: "Yesterday we deployed", not "Yesterday deployed we".

## must not

"Must not" is a prohibition in English. It does not mean "does not have to".
German "muss nicht", Dutch "moet niet" and Czech "nemusí" mean the opposite of
what "must not" says. In specifications this inverts a requirement, and in RFC
2119 wording (MUST, MUST NOT, SHOULD, MAY) it inverts a normative one. Use "does
not have to" or "need not" when no prohibition is meant.

## Numbers and abbreviations

- Decimal separator is a point in English: 3.5, not 3,5. Thousands separator is
  a comma or a narrow gap: 1,000 or 1 000.
- Milliarde, miliarda, miljard, milliard, mil millones, miliardo = billion
  (10^9). The German, Czech, Spanish and Greek word "Billion" is a trillion.
- "resp." (respektive, respectievelijk, rispettivamente) has no English
  equivalent. Rewrite with "and", "or", or two sentences.
- "i.e." means "that is" and "e.g." means "for example". They are not
  interchangeable.
